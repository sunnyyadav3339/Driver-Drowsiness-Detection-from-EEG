Explainable and Interpretable AI for Drowsiness Detection: A Cross-Modal Review and Critical Appraisal

Sunny Yadav, Akshay [surname], and Aryan [surname]

The authors are with the Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee, India. [department, supervisor, e-mail]

Abstract—Deep-learning drowsiness detectors are hard to trust in a vehicle because their decisions are opaque, and interpretable and explainable methods are offered as the remedy. We review 45 papers across electroencephalography (EEG), cardiac signals, camera-based vision, vehicle signals and multimodal fusion, classify them by how the explanation is produced and what it refers to, and appraise 41 detection studies on four checks: agreement with known physiology, a faithfulness test, testing on unseen drivers, and public code. Thirty-two studies report explanations at least partly consistent with known physiology, but only two tested faithfulness quantitatively, 28 did not evaluate the explanation, 15 clearly tested on unseen drivers, and five share code. Where explanations were tested they could disagree: attention and band ablation conflict in one EEG model, and four explainers rank differently on one alertness model. Several labels derive from model inputs or recording sessions, and median reported accuracy is 97.1% under random or within-subject splits against 77.2% on unseen drivers. A case study on a published interpretable EEG model shows that it outputs a constant class when normalisation cannot see the whole test subject, that changing only those statistics alters up to 24.6% of decisions, that attribution methods strongest on deletion are weakest on a randomisation sanity check, and that the reading of alert decisions as artefacts holds weakly on the 11-subject subset but more clearly on all 27 subjects. We close with a reporting checklist. The papers came from targeted searches, so counts describe the examined set, not the field.

Index Terms—Drowsiness detection, driver monitoring, explainable artificial intelligence, interpretability, electroencephalography, heart rate variability, faithfulness, cross-subject validation.

I. Introduction

Drowsy driving remains a major road-safety problem. One recent survey cites a United States estimate attributing up to 20% of annual traffic deaths in 2016 to driver drowsiness [1]. Automated detectors now use brain signals, heart signals, cameras and vehicle data, and reported accuracies often exceed 95% under controlled conditions [2].

High accuracy is not enough for a safety system. A driver, a manufacturer or a regulator needs to know why an alarm fired and whether the model relies on drowsiness itself or on an accident of the training data. The need grows once the estimate acts on the vehicle: recent shared-steering controllers use a fatigue estimate to decide how much authority the automation takes [3, 4]. Interpretable models and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods aim to answer these questions. How well their explanations hold up has received little attention.

Existing reviews leave this gap open. The closest is the PRISMA review of Ajayi et al. [2], which covers 78 fatigue-detection studies published up to April 2025. It found explainability in only 19.2% of them, with SHAP used in six, and treats transparency as part of a roadmap, but it counts the use of XAI without examining what the explanations showed or whether they were valid. Fu et al. [1] survey applications, sensors and methods across modalities, and Ali et al. [5] systematically review brain-imaging detectors; neither has a section on explanation. Recent multimodal surveys treat explainability as a future direction or a supporting tool [6, 7]. Presacan et al. [8] review XAI for EEG across many applications through 2023, but drowsiness receives one paragraph, covering one research group, and modalities other than EEG are out of scope. To our knowledge, no review has focused on interpretability and explainability in drowsiness detection across modalities. [Keep this claim only after the Scopus check. IEEE Xplore and web searches on 2026-11-02 found no such review.]

We address four research questions:

  • RQ1: Which interpretable-by-design and post-hoc methods are used for drowsiness detection, and in which modalities?
  • RQ2: What do the explanations reveal, and do they agree with known physiology and with what the sensors can show?
  • RQ3: How often are explanations tested for faithfulness, how often are models tested on unseen drivers, and what happens when these tests are run?
  • RQ4: What stands in the way of trustworthy explanations in vehicles, and what should future studies report?

Our contributions are:

  1. a review dedicated to interpretability and explainability in drowsiness detection, covering EEG, cardiac, vision, vehicle and multimodal systems;
  2. a taxonomy that classifies each study by the mechanism of its explanation, not the authors' wording, and by what the explanation refers to;
  3. a critical appraisal of 41 detection studies on four checks, with the counts reported per study so that others can verify them, and a synthesis of the cases where explanations were tested and disagreed;
  4. an empirical case study on a published interpretable model, testing its explanations for faithfulness, sanity and stability on every held-out window, and measuring how far its decisions depend on the test protocol; and
  5. a reporting checklist for future studies, including screens for data provenance and for claims that exceed the sensor layout.

Section II gives the background and our review method. Section III presents the taxonomy, Section IV reviews each modality, and Section V summarises the datasets. Section VI appraises the evidence and presents the case study. Section VII discusses challenges and the checklist, and Section VIII concludes.

II. Background and Review Method

A. Signs of drowsiness

An explanation can only be judged against what is already known about drowsiness. Four groups of signs recur in the literature [1, 5].

Brain activity. Theta and alpha power generally rise as alertness falls. Alpha spindles and theta bursts are the transient forms most often linked to drowsiness in the EEG studies reviewed here [9–11].

Heart activity. Heart rate tends to fall with low arousal, and heart rate variability (HRV) measures such as RMSSD, high-frequency power and the LF/HF ratio are read as indicators of autonomic balance [12].

Eyes, face and head. Longer and slower blinks, a higher percentage of eye closure over time (PERCLOS), yawning and head nodding are the standard behavioural signs [1, 13].

Vehicle control. Fewer small steering corrections, sometimes followed by a large correction, can precede a drowsiness-related lane departure [14], and steering variability and lateral motion tend to grow with severe fatigue [15].

B. Interpretable versus explainable

Many papers use the two terms interchangeably [8]. We separate them by mechanism. A method is intrinsic (interpretable by design) when the decision can be read directly from the model: a rule, a threshold, a transparent feature or a model's own internal state. A method is post-hoc (explainable) when a separate computation is run after training to attribute the decision to the input. This follows Rudin's distinction between explaining a black box and using an interpretable model [16]. It also means we sometimes classify a paper differently from how its authors describe it. For example, we treat class activation mapping as post-hoc and model-specific, even when the network was designed to support it, because the map is computed after training.

The two families raise different questions. A post-hoc map may not reflect what the model does, so its faithfulness is in doubt. A rule is faithful by construction, because the rule is the decision, so the open questions are whether a person can actually read it and whether the decision is correct. We keep these questions apart throughout: a model can be transparent and wrong, and a map can be faithful to a model that has learned a shortcut.

C. Review method

This is a structured narrative review, not a systematic review under PRISMA. We searched in two stages. First, we used general academic web search, Europe PMC and arXiv, and checked IEEE Xplore for overlapping reviews. The base query combined three groups of terms: (drowsiness, drowsy, sleepiness, fatigue, vigilance); (driver, driving, driver monitoring); and (explainable, interpretable, XAI, SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM, class activation, saliency, layer-wise relevance, integrated gradients, attention). Searches were repeated with added terms for each modality group. We selected 30 papers, ten per modality group, preferring recent work (2024–2026) and including older papers where they are foundational, and we recorded no screening counts for this stage.

Second, we exported an IEEE Xplore result list on 2 November 2026 and screened its 229 records, of which 45 were judged relevant. From these we added 15 primary studies, four to six per modality group, and the review of Ajayi et al. [2]. [Add the exact IEEE Xplore query and how the 16 were chosen from the 45.] This gives 45 primary papers that use an interpretable model or apply an explanation method to drowsiness or fatigue.

Each paper was read in full by one author, who completed a fixed 12-field template covering data, labels, model, explanation method, findings, results, limitations and four appraisal checks. Key numbers and the strongest critical statements were then checked against the source PDFs. Four papers do not add to the appraisal counts: a methods study [17], a dataset paper with no explanation method [18], a study that classifies driving manoeuvres rather than drowsiness [19], and one of two papers with identical results tables, which we count once (Section IV-C) [20, 21]. That leaves 41 studies, 33 of them from 2024–2026.

Limits of this method. The selection is targeted, not exhaustive, and it includes weak studies deliberately so that we could examine the range of practice. The counts therefore describe the studies we examined and should not be read as prevalence across the field. The second stage drew on one database. Studies are not all independent: several come from the same groups [3, 4, 9, 10, 22–25]. Each paper had one reader, and the appraisal involves judgement; the per-study ratings in Tables II–IV are given so that readers can check them.

III. A Taxonomy of Explanation Methods

Fig. 1 organises the methods along two axes: how the explanation is produced and what it refers to. Table I places the reviewed studies in it. Of the 41 appraised studies, 24 rely on post-hoc methods, 11 are intrinsic, five combine the two, and one claims interpretability without showing any explanation.

A. Intrinsic methods

Rules and logic. Rule-based systems make every decision traceable. Beles et al. [26] fuse physiological and camera cues with fuzzy rules, and Ersoy et al. [27] calibrate eye and mouth thresholds to each driver. Fuzzy systems range widely in how readable they are. Gao et al. [24] stack Takagi–Sugeno–Kang classifiers whose rules use linguistic levels but refer to features only by index, whereas Hu et al. [3] and Yan and Xie [4] write interval type-2 rules over named eye and mouth measures, the latter constraining the membership functions so that the sets stay ordered and distinguishable. Georgiadis et al. [28] go furthest towards symbolic reasoning, with an ontology whose logic rules map physiological thresholds to driver states.

Transparent features. Albadawi et al. [29] feed eye aspect ratio, mouth aspect ratio and head position to simple classifiers, so an alarm can be traced to a named cue.

Model internals. Other studies read explanations from LSTM hidden states [22], attention scores [23, 30] or the eigenvalues of a recurrent weight matrix [31]. Internal states are built in, but that does not establish that they explain the decision. Attention weights in particular need not track input importance [32], and Section VI-C shows that the question is live in this domain.

B. Post-hoc, model-specific methods

Class activation mapping (CAM) [33] weights the last convolutional feature maps by the class weights of a global-average-pooling network. Grad-CAM [34] extends the idea to other architectures using gradients, Grad-CAM++ [35] uses higher-order gradient terms, and LayerCAM [36] weights each location by its positive gradients and works at any layer. This family dominates the vision studies. Layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) [37] passes the output score backwards layer by layer, and integrated gradients [38] accumulate gradients along a path from a baseline to the input. These produce finer-grained maps, and the integrated-gradients result depends on the chosen baseline. Attention rollout [39] combines attention across transformer layers and needs the same testing as other attention maps; one reviewed study applies it across modalities [40].

C. Post-hoc, model-agnostic methods

SHAP [41] assigns each feature a Shapley value and, in the studies reviewed, is applied mostly to tree models on hand-crafted features. LIME [42] fits a simple local surrogate, and occlusion [43] measures the score drop when part of the input is hidden. PGM-Explainer perturbs graph nodes and fits a Bayesian network over them; it appears here in one study on a graph network [44]. Partial dependence [45] and individual conditional expectation plots [46] show how predictions change with a feature, but they assume the feature varies independently of the others, which is doubtful for correlated physiological measures.

D. Beyond attribution

Three other forms appear rarely. McDonald et al. [14] explain a model by inspecting example inputs from each cell of the confusion matrix. Counterfactual explanations [47], the smallest change that would flip a decision, have been proposed for drowsiness alarms [48] but not evaluated. Uncertainty estimates [49] complement an explanation but do not attribute a decision.

IV. Explanations by Modality

Tables II–IV summarise each study. The last column gives the four appraisal ratings: P, explanation agrees with known physiology; E, explanation evaluated; S, tested on unseen drivers; C, code public.

A. EEG and EOG

EEG holds the richest explanation work, and one pattern runs through it: the two classes are explained by different kinds of evidence. From inspecting selected samples, Cui et al. read drowsy decisions as resting on theta bursts and alpha spindles, which match the literature, and alert decisions as resting largely on muscle activity at peripheral channels, eye movements and slow sensor drift [9, 10, 22]. ID3RSNet shows the same split, although its explanations rely less on single blink-like peaks than an earlier compact model [50]. The authors of these studies treat this as a finding and suggest that artefact handling should be a deliberate design choice. If correct, it is also a warning, because artefacts that help on one recording setup may not transfer to other hardware. Section VI-F tests this reading across all windows: support is weak on the 11-subject subset and clearer on all 27 subjects.

Zhuang et al. [11] reach a similar result on the same data with a different model and a different explanation method. In a graph network with a learned connectivity matrix, occlusion maps highlighted alpha spindles in correctly classified drowsy samples for 10 of 11 subjects and beta oscillations in alert samples for all 11, and highlighted segments had significantly higher alpha power when the model predicted drowsy. This is the strongest corroboration of the drowsy reading in the set. The maps themselves were inspected on examples, however, and the learned “connectivity” is a weighting inside the model, so its reduction in occipital and parietal regions describes the network at least as much as the brain.

Seo et al. [58], who removed eye and muscle artefacts with independent component analysis, obtained cortical explanations: LRP on source-space band power highlighted frontal theta and parieto-occipital alpha, and group statistics confirmed the alpha increase. Their model reached 96.9% after fine-tuning on each driver but stayed near chance (50.8%) on unseen drivers. Plausible explanations and cross-subject generalisation are therefore separate properties.

Other studies complicate the theta and alpha story. An et al. [30] read band importance from attention scores: theta and alpha exceeded beta for the three best-predicted subjects, but not consistently for the three worst, and no other subjects were analysed. Jiang et al. [62] used a transparent selection pipeline, nested inside leave-one-subject-out folds, and found eight features selected in every fold. These were amplitude statistics plus beta and gamma power; theta and alpha power were not retained. The labels were task stages in a laboratory task, so a stable feature may track time on task rather than fatigue. Zhou et al. [63] applied no formal XAI method but probed the model through band masking and channel removal; theta and alpha were the strongest narrow bands, although every single band still gave 83–92%. Gao et al. [23] found gamma the most active band under attention and the best with beta when trained alone. Gamma at temporal sites is where muscle contamination is expected, and the paper does not discuss this possibility, so a physiological reading of the band remains open.

Explanations can also claim more than the montage allows. Xu et al. [25] read activation maps from SEED-VIG as evidence about dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the precuneus and the posterior cingulate, but SEED-VIG records only 17 temporal and posterior scalp channels [67], and the maps are shown for three chosen subjects. Gao et al. [31] state that recurrent-matrix poles reveal 6 Hz theta activity, but they report no pole values, and the recurrence runs over 8-s feature windows rather than raw samples, which makes that reading hard to reconcile. Wu et al. [51] use EEG to study hand–foot coordination under fatigue, and LayerCAM on connectivity matrices agrees with their graph statistics on frontal and central connections. Their study shows XAI used for scientific exploration, but a CNN treats adjacent matrix cells as neighbours, and those cells are neighbours only through channel ordering, so regions in the map may depend on that ordering. Their test split is also random over overlapping windows.

Interpretable fuzzy models can also fall short of being readable. Gao et al. [24] conclude that their stacked classifier has good interpretability from example rules such as “the 26th feature is high”, but the rules are never mapped to channels, bands or any physiological claim, so a reader cannot check them. Precision, recall and F1 are also identical within every row of both of its results tables, which should be clarified before the numbers are compared.

Explanations also help audit data. Cui et al. traced errors to sensor noise and to alert-labelled samples full of alpha spindles, which suggests label noise in reaction-time labels [9, 10]. Zhuang et al. similarly link errors to samples where spindles and beta activity co-exist [11].

B. ECG and HRV

Explanation work on cardiac signals consists almost entirely of SHAP on classical models over hand-crafted HRV features. We found no ECG drowsiness model explained by attribution on the raw signal. Vitorino et al. [59] ranked SD2/SD1, a nonlinear HRV measure, as the most consistently important feature, and showed that plausible adversarial perturbations of HRV features sharply degrade most models. They tested the robustness of predictions, not of explanations, which remains open.

Puspasari et al. [12] used subject-grouped validation and report SHAP rankings led by mean RR interval and mean heart rate. Their summary plot illustrates a problem with redundant inputs. The two features are mathematical inverses, yet high values of both push the prediction away from the late-session class, assuming the plotted values refer to that class. High RMSSD also pushes towards it, against the text's account of reduced HRV. Correlated features can thus produce rankings that are hard to read physiologically. In addition, the label is session order, so the explanation describes time on task as much as fatigue.

AlArnaout et al. [66] describe their tree models as easy to interpret but show no explanation. They also label windows from heart-rate thresholds while mean RR is an input feature, although the paper states that labels and inputs are independent.

C. Vision

Vision studies mostly show Grad-CAM heatmaps of eyes and mouth, and the usual evidence is a few images judged by eye. Saxena et al. [52] apply five methods to one model and report how well the maps overlap detected eye and mouth regions, which measures plausibility; their limitations section acknowledges that the explanations were evaluated only qualitatively, with no faithfulness assessment. Rafy et al. [53] give the most instructive result among the heatmap studies: maps on the realistic UTA-RLDD data partly fall on background regions, and focus metrics drop as recording conditions become less controlled, so the explanation exposes a dataset effect that accuracy conceals. Oise et al. [49] name Grad-CAM++ and uncertainty as ensuring transparency, but the paper contains no explanation output, and its figures do not match its headline metrics. Jewel et al. [57] report eyelid and pupil maps as fostering trust without analysing them, and their 99.28% on UTA-RLDD follows image shuffling that they describe as preventing subject bias, although shuffling before a split places frames of one person on both sides of it.

Zeynalpour et al. [44] stand apart. They apply SHAP, occlusion, PGM-Explainer and Grad-CAM to a graph network over body and face keypoints and score each with fidelity measures across sparsity levels. The methods agree that facial keypoints, especially the eyes, drive not-alert predictions, but disagree on alert ones: SHAP and PGM-Explainer favour hands and upper body, occlusion mostly the face, and Grad-CAM spreads across both. Section VI-C returns to the fidelity scores. The setting is limited, with train-driver states acted at a desk and two test participants, so hand movement may reflect instructions to mimic driving rather than alertness, and the study does not report the model's accuracy.

Florez et al. [55, 64] use Grad-CAM on a handful of images to choose between networks of almost equal accuracy, even though many maps fall on the nose, cheek or forehead. Explanations here steer model selection without having been tested. Nomura et al. [54] use near-infrared face images and find activation mainly around the eyes, with additional responses along the nose, mouth and forehead, which they link to blood-flow changes. They note that eye activation may reflect blinking; masking the eyes would test this, but was not done.

A subtler issue is that many vision labels are really eye-state labels. When the class is simply open versus closed, as in Hassan et al. [56] and the Florez studies, a heatmap on the eyelid confirms the label definition and says little about drowsiness. Rule-based alternatives avoid opacity [27, 29], and personalising thresholds narrowed part of the gap to a CNN in one preprint [27], but these too need independent labels. In the study of Albadawi et al., the labels are generated from thresholds on the same features the classifier receives.

Two further rule systems feed a controller. Hu et al. [3] and Yan and Xie [4] turn eye and mouth openness into a fatigue level through interval type-2 fuzzy rules and use that level to set steering authority. Their rules are the most readable in the set, and they report control gains with three simulator drivers, but neither checks the fatigue estimate against any independent measure of drowsiness.

Finally, two conference papers with different author lists [20, 21] report identical results tables at all three train–test ratios, with a maximum of 98.87%, share long runs of text and equations, and name no dataset, subject count or labelling procedure. We count them as one study, rate that study on what is reported, and do not rely on its numbers.

D. Vehicle-based signals

Vehicle signals remain the least explored. McDonald et al. [14] explain a steering-based random forest by inspecting example traces: low-variability “steering holds” count as drowsy, and ordinary low-activity driving causes false alarms. The authors acknowledge that it is unclear which features the forest uses, and variable importance was not reported. Zhu et al. [15] give the clearest vehicle-only explanation in the set. SHAP on a steering-signal random forest shows steering-angle variability and lateral acceleration rising with severe fatigue, while feature values near their medians point to mild fatigue, the class most often confused. That account of where the method is weak is useful, but SHAP is also used to select the features, the paper describes no nesting of selection inside folds, and windows are not grouped by driver, so the 93.71% is likely optimistic. The sample is also all male students. Zhou et al. [60] include steering among SHAP-ranked features, where it ranks below heart and breathing measures, although random splitting of one-second samples makes their accuracy hard to interpret. We found no vehicle-only deep model with formal attribution in our set.

E. Multimodal fusion

Fusion raises a question that single-signal work does not: how much does each modality contribute? The studies here rarely answer it. Hasan et al. [13] come closest among the verifiable studies, finding that blink features and central theta outrank HRV under SHAP and partial dependence, and they report the strongest validation in this group, comparing six schemes on the same data and arguing that leave-one-participant-out gives the most general results. Zhang et al. [65] interpret a deep EEG–EOG model through band correlations and t-SNE but do not attribute the fused prediction; because EOG measures blinks directly and the label is eye-tracker PERCLOS, the EOG branch may sit close to the label itself. Summing SHAP values by modality is also misleading when modalities differ in feature count, as Askari et al. [19] partly concede.

Guan et al. [61] hold out ten unseen drivers and reach 94.28% across normal, distracted and fatigued states, and SHAP ranks wrist-motion and vehicle-dynamics features above HRV and skin conductance. Here the explanation is most useful as a warning. Each state was a separate timed session, with a movie on a tablet in the distraction session and a set speed range in the fatigue session, so the leading features may separate the task in each session rather than the driver's internal state. The authors also describe SHAP as yielding a causal feature weighting and name DeepExplainer for a tree ensemble; SHAP is not causal, and TreeExplainer would be the expected choice.

Theivadas and Ponnan [40] present a design aimed directly at the modality question: a dashboard of attention over time, in which behavioural cues dominate when alert and physiological cues rise at drowsiness onset. We cite this as a design idea and cannot verify its results. Its top physiological features include skin-conductance level, which none of its three described datasets is said to record; we could not locate a source for “SIM-DROWSY”, whose citation number falls beyond the printed reference list; and the “expert annotations” behind its consistency score are not described. Beles et al. [26] offer readable rules but no quantitative test on synchronised data, and Jha et al. [48] propose counterfactual advice without reported data.

F. Knowledge-based reasoning

Georgiadis et al. [28] take the opposite position from the black-box models. An ontology with logic rules classifies heart rate, HRV, respiration and SpO2 into threshold categories personalised by age and sex and infers states such as drowsiness suspected, so every inference can be traced to explicit rules. Its evaluation covers logical consistency and competency questions on pilot data augmented with synthetic series, and the authors say plainly that this is neither a real-world benchmark nor clinically definitive. Drowsiness level is also an input from elsewhere, so the system explains how evidence is combined, not how drowsiness is detected. Together with the fuzzy controllers above [3, 4], it marks one end of a trade-off in the reviewed set: full transparency with no evidence of correct detection.

V. Datasets

Table V lists the public datasets used by the reviewed studies. Three features matter for explanation. First, the evidence base is narrow. Twelve of the 15 EEG papers use the Cao et al. lane-departure dataset [68] or SEED-VIG [67]. Eight use Cao, and seven of those use the same 11-subject subset, so many claims about what EEG models learn rest on one simulator, one labelling rule and about eleven drivers.

Second, datasets differ in what their labels mean: reaction time, PERCLOS, self-report, observer rating, eye state or the recording session. An explanation can only be as meaningful as the label it explains. MRL Eye and CEW, for instance, record whether the eye is open and do not measure drowsiness at all.

Third, a dataset limits what an explanation can claim. SEED-VIG has temporal and posterior channels only, so it cannot directly support statements about prefrontal activity [25], and any band finding at its temporal sites has to contend with muscle activity [23]. Studies also use differing subsets of SEED-VIG, including 14, 21 and 23 subjects [23, 24, 63], which complicates comparison between them.

Many studies use private data [12, 13, 15, 24, 44, 51, 54, 58, 60–62], which prevents independent checks of their explanations. Others name no dataset [20, 21, 48] or one we could not trace [40].

VI. Do the Explanations Hold Up?

A. How explanations can be tested

Agreement with expectations does not show that an explanation reflects the model. Faithfulness tests ask whether the features a method marks as important actually drive the output. Deletion tests remove top-ranked inputs and track the predicted probability. ROAR removes them and retrains, which avoids the distribution shift of plain deletion [75]. Sanity checks randomise model weights or labels, which should destroy a faithful map; Adebayo et al. showed that some popular saliency methods barely change [76]. Simulated EEG with known class-relevant signals provides a ground truth that real data lack [77].

These tests matter in this domain. Cui et al. [17] compared seven attribution methods against random maps on three EEG datasets, including the Cao subset. Gradient×input, DeepLIFT, integrated gradients and LRP beat the random baseline, whereas saliency maps, deconvolution and guided backpropagation mostly did not. Even the better methods produced misleading maps for some samples: deleting the channel marked most important raised one prediction from 0.6 to 0.98. Their study covers gradient-based methods and two shallow networks, so it does not settle the question for CAM, SHAP or attention, which most reviewed papers use. Fidelity measures offer a similar test for graph models: Fidelity+ measures the prediction drop when top-ranked nodes are removed (necessity), and Fidelity- the change when only those nodes are kept (sufficiency) [44].

B. Appraisal of the 41 studies

Fig. 2 shows the result. Thirty-two studies report explanations that at least partly agree with known physiology, but this is the weakest check. For eye-state tasks the agreement holds by definition, in rule systems it holds by construction [3, 4, 28], and several claims rest on a few chosen images.

Only two studies tested faithfulness quantitatively. Feng et al. [50] used perturbation sensitivity and deletion tests, comparing against one other model without a random baseline, and Zeynalpour et al. [44] scored four explainers on fidelity. Eleven studies applied a partial check, of three kinds. Plausibility measures whether maps land where expected or match stated expert views [40, 52, 53]. Consistency compares methods or resamples: SHAP against partial dependence on the same model (r = 0.849) [13], selection stability across folds [62], and bootstrap aggregation [12]. Sufficiency shows that top-ranked features or bands retrain to a similar score [11, 15, 23, 59, 60]. These checks are useful, but none shows that the explanation reflects what the model does. Agreement between two methods on one model is consistency, and a stable feature can still be a shortcut. The remaining 28 studies did not evaluate the explanation.

Only 15 of the 41 studies clearly tested on unseen drivers, a further five state it without enough detail to verify, and three rule systems have no detection test at all. Five share code. Only two studies combine a faithfulness test with testing on unseen drivers, one of them with two test participants [44], and the other's authors still read alertness partly as muscle and drift signals [50]. Passing a faithfulness test does not rule out a shortcut.

C. When explanations are tested, they can disagree

The few studies that test explanations give the review's most direct evidence that a displayed explanation cannot be taken at face value.

Attention against retraining. Two EEG papers compare attention rankings with models retrained on one band at a time, with opposite outcomes. In Zhuang et al. [11] theta had the highest attention score and gave the best single-band model (72.6%), and beta was lowest on both. In Gao et al. [23] delta ranked high under attention but had the least effect when trained alone, a conflict the authors acknowledge. In the same paper, removing the attention module did not significantly change accuracy, and the module was kept partly for interpretability. Attention therefore can agree with a test or fail it, in line with the wider caution about attention as explanation [32]. Single-band retraining also has a limit: it shows what a band can achieve alone in a new model, not what the original model uses, so even the agreement in Zhuang et al. is indirect.

Explainers against each other. Zeynalpour et al. [44] found that the choice of explainer changes both the story and the score. SHAP had the highest Fidelity+ at every sparsity level, with its peak at a sparsity of 0.6, PGM-Explainer gave the most sufficient subsets, and Grad-CAM, the method used by most vision papers in this review, scored low on Fidelity+. The method that best identifies necessary inputs is therefore not the one that best identifies sufficient inputs. Two cautions apply. Explanations were computed only for correct predictions, and zeroing a keypoint's relative coordinates moves it to the nose, which is an unrealistic input and may exaggerate fidelity.

Explanations against the data. A further group of problems appears without any formal test, by comparing explanations with what the recording could contain: anatomical claims beyond the montage [25], salient features not among the described signals [40], and regions that depend on the order of channels in a matrix [51]. Our case study in Section VI-F adds that the same methods can rank in opposite order on deletion and sanity tests.

D. Accuracy depends on the test scheme

An explanation of a model that has memorised its test drivers has limited value, so the test scheme bears directly on RQ3. Fig. 3 plots reported accuracy by scheme. The median is 97.1% under random or within-subject splits and 77.2% on unseen drivers. The tasks and datasets differ, so this comparison is only descriptive, but the same pattern appears within single studies: 96.9% to 50.8% for Seo et al. [58], 97.47% to 85.43% for Zhou et al. [63], and 97.2% to 92.4% for Rafy et al. when moving to another dataset [53]. On UTA-RLDD, a subject-independent baseline reached 65.2% at video level, with human observers at 57.8% [18]; later papers report 97–99% on the same data with image-level splits, although on a simpler two-class task [53, 57].

The effect is not uniform. Gao et al. [23] lose little between five-fold testing and leave-one-subject-out (87.13% to 85.3%). In Hasan et al. [13], leave-one-participant-out reached 80.1%, close to a single hold-out split (81.3%) and above their noisy 10-fold results, on a small dataset of 156 observations. Nor is an unseen-driver test sufficient: Guan et al. [61] reach 94.28% on ten unseen drivers, but their labels are timed sessions, so the test protects against memorising people and does not protect against learning the session. Variance also matters: subject-level means can hide failure on individual drivers, such as the 47.3% worst case behind a 90.7% mean in Nomura et al. [54].

Selection can leak as well as splitting. Several EEG results report the best epoch selected on test-fold accuracy [9, 10, 22, 50], which is mildly optimistic. Using SHAP to choose features brings the explanation into the model, and unless selection sits inside each training fold the score is biased [15]. The nested design of Jiang et al. [62] shows the alternative.

E. Explanations inherit the label

Labels limit what any explanation can mean. Six recurring problems appear in the reviewed set.

Labels derived from model inputs. AlArnaout et al. [66] label windows using heart-rate change and level, while mean RR, the inverse of heart rate, is an input. Albadawi et al. [29] generate labels from thresholds on the same eye, mouth and head features the classifier receives. High accuracy then partly measures whether the model can recover the rule.

Eye state as drowsiness. When the label is open versus closed [27, 55, 56, 64], a heatmap on the eye confirms the definition.

Time on task as fatigue. Labels from session order [12] or task stage [62] cannot separate fatigue from anything else that changes over a session.

Sessions as states. When each class is a separate timed session [61], time of day, instructions and the task itself are bound to the label, and an explanation can reveal this but cannot remove it.

Eye signals in the input and the label. PERCLOS labels combined with EOG input or without eye-artefact removal [31, 63, 65] may let a model detect eye closure directly. Zhou et al. raise this risk themselves and test it through frontal-channel removal [63].

No independent label. Some systems have acted states [44], synthetic data [28] or no drowsiness measure against which to check the estimate [3, 4], so an explanation can only describe the system's own logic.

By contrast, McDonald et al. [14] use video-rated drowsiness with a steering-only model, and Nomura et al. [54] and Ghoddoosian et al. [18] use ratings independent of the model's computed features. These give their explanations a firmer footing.

F. Case study: testing one interpretable model and its explanations

Published accuracy for interpretable EEG models often assumes a protocol that a vehicle cannot follow. Cui et al. note that their compact model's batch normalisation uses the whole test subject at once, and that about 6% of its accuracy depends on this [10]. We measured the effect on the multi-channel InterpretableCNN [9], using our own implementation of its training recipe on the balanced 11-subject subset (2,022 samples) under leave-one-subject-out, with five seeds.

Table VIII shows the results. With statistics from the whole held-out subject, the model reaches 77.93%, close to the published 78.35%. Scoring windows one at a time with the same design, as a live system must, gives a constant output (50.00%, AUC 0.498). Training with running statistics restores a usable streaming model at 73.68%, which is 4.25 points below the whole-subject figure (better on 9 of 11 subjects for the whole-subject protocol, Wilcoxon p = 0.042). On all 27 subjects of the original dataset, using our own conversion of the data, the streaming model reaches 73.59% balanced accuracy and an AUC of 0.828, so the figure is not specific to the subset.

For comparison, the deep model DeltaGateNet reports 83.21% on this subset with subject-level five-fold splits [78]. Under leave-one-subject-out we obtained 64.74%, but this is a single seed and a different split, so it is preliminary and is not a direct replication.

Attribution tests. We then tested explanations of the two InterpretableCNN variants on every held-out window (five seeds, 11 subjects; all tests across subjects are two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests). Retraining reproduced the accuracies in Table VIII exactly. We compared saliency, gradient×input, integrated gradients (zero baseline, 50 steps) and CAM with CNN-Fixation tracing, the method published with this model [9], each explaining the logit margin of the predicted class with normalisation statistics held fixed. The peripheral channel set and the frequency bands were fixed before any results were seen. Code and per-subject results are at https://github.com/sunnyyadav3339/Driver-Drowsiness-Detection-from-EEG. [True only after research/ is pushed.]

The decision depends on other windows. A heatmap explains one window, but under whole-subject normalisation the decision also depends on the driver's other windows. Table VI measures this by rescoring the same windows with the same weights and changing only the statistics; labels were used only to build the contexts. Statistics from 30 random windows nearly reproduce the whole-subject result, with 4.80% of predictions changing. Statistics from alert windows alone change 24.55% of predictions and raise the predicted-drowsy share from 45.59% to 68.21%, and the maps drift far from the baseline (similarity 0.502). A sample-level heatmap shows none of this dependence.

Faithfulness and sanity can disagree. Table VII and Fig. 4 show that every method beat random deletion on at least 10 of 11 subjects (p ≤ 0.002), including the published tracing method, which the EEG comparison of Cui et al. [17] did not evaluate. Gradient×input and integrated gradients were strongest under point deletion. This result needs caution: in a nearly piecewise-linear network, removing the points with the largest positive first-order contribution should lower the margin almost by construction, and zeroing takes EEG outside its normal distribution. Channel deletion is more conservative, and there the gap narrows (0.50 against 0.56 for tracing). The same two methods are the weakest on the sanity check: their maps keep a rank correlation of about 0.51 with maps from a randomly initialised network, against 0.13 for saliency and 0.17 for tracing. Multiplying by the input carries amplitude structure into the map whatever the weights are, as Adebayo et al. caution [76]. Saliency beat random here, unlike in Cui et al. [17], although the procedures differ. No method wins on every test, so reporting one test alone can mislead. Maps were also only moderately stable: channel maps from identical data and different seeds correlated at 0.56 for tracing and 0.72–0.75 for the others.

What the model uses. Fig. 4(c) and Fig. 5 give the all-window picture for the whole-subject model. Removing theta, alpha or delta lowered P(drowsy) by 0.24, 0.17 and 0.09 on all 11 subjects (p = 0.001), and zeroing the 15 central channels lowered it by 0.41. This supports the drowsy reading. Spatially, tracing also agrees with the published alert reading: it placed 64.0% of alert relevance on the 15 peripheral channels against 45.6% for drowsy (p = 0.007), peaking at F7 for alert and at PZ and CPZ for drowsy. Gradient×input shows a smaller difference (51.9% against 46.2%, p = 0.010).

On this subset, perturbation does not confirm the alert reading. Zeroing the peripheral channels barely changed P(alert) (-0.007, p = 0.58), and removing beta had no significant effect (+0.008, p = 0.28); only gamma removal lowered it, slightly (-0.036, p = 0.001). Across all windows of this subset, the model behaves like a detector of central low-frequency evidence, with alertness close to the absence of that evidence plus a small high-frequency contribution. Results for the running-statistics variant are similar. These findings qualify the artefact reading but do not refute it. Removing signal also lowers its energy, which may itself favour the alert class. Muscle activity is broadband, so band removal cannot isolate it, and averages can hide windows that behave as the published examples do. The analysis covers one model and 11 subjects, and we did not correct for multiple comparisons, so it should be treated as descriptive evidence.

The full dataset gives a clearer answer. We repeated the removal tests with the running-statistics model on all 27 subjects of the original dataset, using our own conversion with natural class balance (five seeds, labelled trials from the 31st of each session). Zeroing the peripheral channels lowered P(alert) by 0.110 (p < 0.001), and removing gamma lowered it by 0.074 (p < 0.001). The artefact reading therefore has more support on the full dataset than on the 11-subject subset. Here too, removing theta lowered P(drowsy) most among the bands (0.244). The general point stands: a conclusion drawn from inspected samples should be tested across all windows, and here both the test and the choice of data change the conclusion.

VII. Challenges and Future Directions

Artefacts and shortcuts. In the EEG studies we examined, authors repeatedly read alert decisions as muscle, eye and drift signals, and our all-window tests give weak support on the 11-subject subset and clearer support on all 27 subjects (Section VI-F). Occlusion evidence for beta in alert decisions [11] and gamma-led attention at temporal sites [23] add to the question, since both are hard to separate from muscle activity without further tests. Future work should test whether a model survives artefact removal, a different sensor or a new site, and should state which signals it is meant to use. Comparing explanations with and without artefact removal would be a direct test.

Testing attention and other built-in explanations. Attention and learned graphs are now a common interpretability claim in recent EEG work [11, 23, 25, 30], and the one direct conflict we found [23] shows why they should not be reported as importance without a test. Occluding the attended bands or channels in the original model would answer this more directly than retraining on single bands.

Better tests for each modality. Deletion by zeroing pushes EEG out of its normal distribution, zeroing keypoints creates unrealistic poses [44], and baselines for integrated gradients are a design choice. Vision has a cheap test that none of the reviewed papers ran: mask the eyes and check whether accuracy holds, which would directly test claims of a physiological signal beyond eye closure [54]. Randomisation sanity checks [76] are also cheap and were absent from every reviewed study. Our case study and the fidelity comparison of Zeynalpour et al. [44] both show that methods rank differently on different tests, so more than one test should be reported.

Stability across drivers. A useful explanation should hold on drivers the model has not seen. Most studies explain a few correctly classified samples, or class averages [23, 51]. Few report explanation results across all subjects or for errors, and the robustness of explanations under perturbation is untested [59].

Attribution for raw cardiac and vehicle signals. We found no attribution on raw ECG or steering signals in the reviewed set. Redundant HRV features complicate SHAP, so grouping correlated features, or removing them before explanation, should be standard. Vehicle-only explanation is promising but has not yet been shown on unseen drivers [15].

Multimodal attribution. Fusion models should report how much each modality contributes, in a way that does not simply reward the modality with more features. Attention over modalities is a natural display [40], but no verifiable study yet tests it, and modality ablation has not been connected to SHAP rankings [61].

Transparent models as a real alternative. Following Rudin [16], rule-based and personalised threshold systems deserve serious evaluation. Personalising thresholds improved eye and yawn classification by 1.3–1.5 points in one preprint, narrowing part of the gap to a CNN [27]. Interpretability constraints on fuzzy sets [4] make readability checkable, and could be applied to fuzzy classifiers whose rules are currently unreadable [24]. Ontologies add traceable reasoning [28]. All of these still need valid labels and quantitative tests on real drowsiness, because no reviewed study offers both a readable decision and a verified detection result on unseen drivers.

Explanations in the control loop. Once a fatigue estimate sets steering authority [3, 4], an error changes the vehicle's behaviour and not only an alarm. This strengthens the case for readable estimators, but it also raises the bar, because an unvalidated estimate cannot be made safe by being readable. Control studies should validate the estimator separately, on more than a few drivers, and report how the controller behaves when the estimate is wrong.

Evidence quality and provenance. Some of the literature cannot be used as evidence at all. We found papers that report no dataset [48], whose figures disagree with their headline metrics [49], whose reported features are not among the described signals [40], or that report identical results tables under different author lists and no named dataset [20, 21]. Reviews that only count XAI use would count all of these. Screening for data provenance and duplicated results should precede any count.

People and deployment. None of the reviewed studies shows, with data, that explanations improve driver or expert understanding or trust. One paper reports a gain in trust without describing a user study [48]. Counterfactual advice also needs care in this domain, because telling a driver to blink less in order to avoid an alarm could teach them to mask genuine drowsiness. Running an explanation on embedded hardware adds latency [53], and several authors note that demographic coverage is thin or unreported [15, 49, 53, 61]. Drowsiness data are also sensitive personal and physiological information, as Ali et al. discuss [5].

VIII. Conclusion

This review examined interpretable and explainable drowsiness detection across modalities. On RQ1, post-hoc methods dominate: the CAM family in vision and single-channel EEG, and SHAP on hand-crafted features in cardiac, vehicle and multimodal work. Intrinsic approaches are growing, from attention and learned graphs in EEG to fuzzy rules and ontologies, but they range from readable rules to rules that no reader can check.

On RQ2, explanations often recover known signs of drowsiness, such as theta and alpha activity, eye closure and changed steering activity. They also point to possible shortcuts: muscle and eye artefacts in EEG, a reading our all-window tests support more clearly on all 27 subjects than on the 11-subject subset, background regions in vision, and session-specific movement in multimodal data. In several studies, what the explanation shows follows from how the label was defined, and some claims go beyond what the sensors can show.

On RQ3, testing is rare. Of the 41 detection studies we appraised, two tested faithfulness quantitatively, 11 applied a partial check and 28 did not evaluate the explanation, while 15 clearly tested on unseen drivers. When tests were run, they could expose disagreement: attention against single-band retraining in one of two EEG studies, and explainers against each other. Our case study adds that a test protocol can decide whether a published interpretable model works at all when scored live, that changing only normalisation statistics can alter a quarter of its decisions, and that deletion and sanity checks can rank the same methods in opposite order.

On RQ4, the main obstacles are untested explanations, labels tied to model inputs or sessions, narrow datasets, unverifiable data and the absence of user studies. They matter more as fatigue estimates begin to act on the vehicle. Our checklist targets these directly. The main limit of this review is that it covers a targeted selection of 45 papers, so a larger systematic count is a natural next step. Until explanations are tested as rigorously as accuracy, a heatmap or a rule should be treated as a hypothesis about a model, not as evidence that the model can be trusted.
